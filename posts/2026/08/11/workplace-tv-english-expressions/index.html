Classic Workplace Expressions from American and British TV Shows (with Explanations)

Collected from 4 classic workplace TV shows: The Office / Suits / Ted Lasso / Succession Each entry includes: the original line / a plain-English paraphrase / what the speaker really means / when to use it 4 shows / 98 expressions

  • The Office (American workplace comedy set at the Dunder Mifflin paper company): Michael Scott's school of awkward management
  • Suits (legal drama set at Pearson Hardman): Harvey Specter's art of hardball negotiation
  • Ted Lasso (sports workplace comedy about AFC Richmond): the philosophy of American coach Ted
  • Succession (family business drama about Waystar Royco): Logan Roy's language of power

The Office

20 classic expressions

1. OK, hear me out.

Paraphrase: Okay, hear me out.

What it really means: I have an idea, plan, or request; please give me a moment to explain.

When to use it: Opening a point in a meeting

2. Bottom line.

Paraphrase: In a nutshell / the core point is.

What it really means: Skip the details and get to the conclusion.

When to use it: Speaking in meetings

3. If you could all just give me one minute of your time.

Paraphrase: Please give me a minute.

What it really means: A polite request for attention, often used when pitching or explaining.

When to use it: Speaking in meetings

4. I’m going to be honest with you.

Paraphrase: I'll be honest with you.

What it really means: What I am about to say may be unwelcome or controversial, but I mean it.

When to use it: Speaking in meetings

5. That’s what she said.

Paraphrase: (That's what she said.)

What it really means: Michael's catchphrase, built on a double entendre (warning: extremely unprofessional; use with care).

When to use it: Speaking in meetings

6. Would you rather be feared or loved? Be loved, it’s easier.

Paraphrase: Would you rather be feared or loved? Loved, because it's easier.

What it really means: A leadership philosophy: real authority comes from respect, not fear.

When to use it: Negotiation and persuasion

7. People will forget what you said, but they’ll never forget how you made them feel.

Paraphrase: People forget what you said, but never how you made them feel.

What it really means: Emotional connection matters more than information. A classic Maya Angelou idea.

When to use it: Negotiation and persuasion

8. I’m not gonna do that.

Paraphrase: I'm not doing that.

What it really means: A direct refusal that draws a boundary. In business, "I'm afraid I can't do that" is softer.

When to use it: Saying no

9. That’s not gonna fly.

Paraphrase: That won't work / that's a no.

What it really means: A short, firm rejection of a proposal or idea.

When to use it: Saying no

10. Way to go, tiger.

Paraphrase: Nice work, tiger!

What it really means: Light verbal praise with a touch of teasing.

When to use it: Praise

11. You killed it out there.

Paraphrase: You were amazing just now.

What it really means: Recognizes someone's performance at an important moment, such as a talk or negotiation.

When to use it: Praise

12. Could you maybe take it down a notch?

Paraphrase: Could you bring it down a notch?

What it really means: A polite request for someone to tone it down.

When to use it: Criticism and feedback

13. Let’s keep this professional.

Paraphrase: Let's keep this professional.

What it really means: A reminder not to bring personal emotion into the situation.

When to use it: Criticism and feedback

14. I’m fine. Everything’s fine.

Paraphrase: I'm fine. Everything's fine.

What it really means: Calm on the surface, possibly under enormous pressure underneath (dark humor).

When to use it: Responding to pressure

15. He’s not even in the room, is he?

Paraphrase: He isn't even in the room, right?

What it really means: Mocks someone for showing off or taking over the conversation (a dig at Dwight).

When to use it: Office politics

16. It’s not a job, it’s a family.

Paraphrase: This isn't just a job; it's a family.

What it really means: Emphasizes belonging (ironically, overpromising loyalty at work can be dangerous).

When to use it: Leaving or retaining staff

17. I wish there was a way to know you’re in the good old days before you’ve actually left them.

Paraphrase: I wish there were a way to know you're in the good old days while you're in them.

What it really means: A classic reminder to cherish the present, quoted in Andy's farewell speech.

When to use it: Leaving or retaining staff

18. So, here’s the deal…

Paraphrase: Here's the situation...

What it really means: Opening a meeting and preparing to explain what is going on.

When to use it: Opening a report or update

19. Any questions? No? Great. Meeting adjourned.

Paraphrase: Questions? No? Great, meeting adjourned.

What it really means: A classic Michael-style meeting wrap-up.

When to use it: Ending a meeting

20. We’re a team. We stick together.

Paraphrase: We're a team, and we need to stick together.

What it really means: Emphasizes team spirit and mutual support.

When to use it: Teamwork

Suits

30 classic expressions

1. I’m not here to make friends. I’m here to win.

Paraphrase: I'm not here to make friends; I'm here to win.

What it really means: Harvey's motto and the harsh truth of business negotiation.

When to use it: Opening a negotiation

2. When you’re backed against the wall, break the wall.

Paraphrase: When you're backed against the wall, break the wall down.

What it really means: Don't compromise in a desperate situation; take the initiative.

When to use it: Negotiation

3. Everyone has a weakness. Find theirs.

Paraphrase: Everyone has a weakness. Find it.

What it really means: Negotiation strategy: you win by understanding your opponent.

When to use it: Negotiation

4. Don’t raise your voice. Improve your argument.

Paraphrase: Don't raise your voice; improve your argument.

What it really means: Let facts and logic do the talking, not emotion.

When to use it: Negotiation

5. First impressions aren’t everything. But they’re the last thing I change my mind about.

Paraphrase: First impressions aren't everything, but they're the last thing to change.

What it really means: First impressions shape how others see you and are hard to overturn.

When to use it: Negotiation

6. Anyone can do my job, but no one can be me.

Paraphrase: Anyone can do my job, but no one can be me.

What it really means: A confident statement that your uniqueness cannot be replaced.

When to use it: Negotiation

7. I’m not going to lie to you. I’m not.

Paraphrase: I won't lie to you. I won't.

What it really means: A strong trust-building opener, especially effective at the start of a negotiation.

When to use it: Negotiation

8. Loyalty is a two-way street. If you’re asking for my loyalty, you’re giving it.

Paraphrase: Loyalty is a two-way street. If you want mine, you have to give yours.

What it really means: Relationships are mutual; one-sided demands do not work.

When to use it: Negotiation

9. Let’s make this simple.

Paraphrase: Let's keep this simple.

What it really means: End a long discussion and get to the real negotiation.

When to use it: Closing a negotiation

10. I’m not going to be pushed. I’m not going to be bullied.

Paraphrase: I won't be pushed around, and I won't be bullied.

What it really means: A Jessica-style strong position that draws a clear boundary.

When to use it: Saying no and setting boundaries

11. That’s not a threat, that’s a promise.

Paraphrase: That's not a threat; it's a promise.

What it really means: I will do what I say. The strongest kind of warning.

When to use it: Saying no

12. If you’re going to screw me, do it to my face.

Paraphrase: If you come after me, do it to my face.

What it really means: I won't accept a stab in the back; if you're going to do it, do it openly.

When to use it: Saying no

13. I don’t get tired. I get results.

Paraphrase: I don't get tired. I get results.

What it really means: A workaholic expression of confidence.

When to use it: Responding to pressure

14. Winners don’t make excuses when the other side plays the game.

Paraphrase: Winners don't make excuses when the other side plays the game.

What it really means: Losers complain about the rules; winners accept them and win.

When to use it: Responding to pressure

15. I play to win.

Paraphrase: I play to win.

What it really means: A clear statement of intent with no middle ground.

When to use it: Responding to pressure

16. You’re not stupid. You just don’t think.

Paraphrase: You're not stupid; you're just not thinking.

What it really means: Sharper criticism: your ability is fine, but your approach needs to change.

When to use it: Praise and encouragement

17. You’ve got potential. Don’t waste it.

Paraphrase: You have potential. Don't waste it.

What it really means: Direct but encouraging feedback.

When to use it: Praise

18. That’s beneath you. And if it isn’t, it should be.

Paraphrase: This is beneath you. If it isn't, it should be.

What it really means: Criticism: you should not be doing this, and you should not lower your standards.

When to use it: Criticism

19. You’re better than this.

Paraphrase: You're better than this / you can do better.

What it really means: The most effective criticism: one sentence that makes someone both embarrassed and grateful.

When to use it: Criticism

20. I expect more from you.

Paraphrase: I expect more from you.

What it really means: Criticism paired with expectations, which signals trust.

When to use it: Criticism

21. The only time success comes before work is in the dictionary.

Paraphrase: The only place success comes before work is in the dictionary.

What it really means: Work comes before success. A classic line.

When to use it: Office politics

22. You always have a choice.

Paraphrase: You always have a choice.

What it really means: A reminder to the other side: no one forced you, so don't make excuses.

When to use it: Office politics

23. I don’t have dreams. I have goals.

Paraphrase: I don't have dreams. I have goals.

What it really means: Not romanticism, but execution and results.

When to use it: Leaving or changing jobs

24. I’m going to walk out that door and never look back.

Paraphrase: I'll walk out that door and never look back.

What it really means: The most determined resignation statement.

When to use it: Leaving a job

25. I don’t play the odds. I play the man.

Paraphrase: I don't play the odds; I play the man.

What it really means: The heart of negotiation: understanding your opponent matters more than calculating odds.

When to use it: Managing up and negotiation

26. Let’s get down to business.

Paraphrase: Let's get down to business.

What it really means: A concise, forceful way to open a formal meeting.

When to use it: Opening a meeting

27. Let’s go to work.

Paraphrase: Let's get to work.

What it really means: Harvey's classic exit line from a meeting: a call to battle.

When to use it: Ending a meeting

28. This firm is my family. And I protect my family.

Paraphrase: This firm is my family, and I protect my family.

What it really means: Expresses loyalty and responsibility to the organization.

When to use it: Teams

29. If I wanted to befriend you, I’d be a better person.

Paraphrase: If I wanted to be your friend, I'd be a better person.

What it really means: In business, I am not here to make friends.

When to use it: Negotiation

30. I’m going to pretend you didn’t just say that.

Paraphrase: I'll pretend you didn't just say that.

What it really means: Declines to respond to an absurd remark and gives the other person a way out.

When to use it: Saying no

Speaking in everyday meetingsThe Office's ‘hear me out’ + Suits' ‘let’s get down to business’
Opening an email or message‘I’d like to add to the discussion.’ (Succession): formal business tone
Declining unreasonable requests‘I’m not gonna do that.’ (Office) or ‘That’s not a threat, that’s a promise.’ (Suits)
Praising a colleague‘You killed it.’ (Office): short and strong
Giving feedback to a direct report‘You’re better than this.’ (Suits): polished and respectful
Reporting upward‘Show me the numbers.’ (Succession): data-driven
When team morale is low‘Be a goldfish.’ (Ted Lasso): short and memorable
Negotiating or nudging a client‘Don’t raise your voice. Improve your argument.’ (Suits): a calm counterpunch
Leaving or changing jobs‘I don’t have dreams. I have goals.’ (Suits): expressing determination
Everyday self-deprecating stress relief‘Fart, then smile. Nobody will suspect.’ (Ted Lasso): dark humor

Notes

  • Sources: Compiled from publicly available show material and widely quoted lines
  • Extreme profanity is not included verbatim: for example, Succession's ‘I don’t give a fuck’ is paired with a milder alternative (‘That’s not a thing.’)
  • How to practice: Before a meeting, pick 1-2 lines, practice them until they feel natural, and use them naturally rather than reciting them mechanically
  • Put it into practice: Watching 5 episodes of one show is more effective than reading 50 quotes
