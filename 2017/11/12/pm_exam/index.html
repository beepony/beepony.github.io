    • Thoughts on the Software Qualification Exam Before the Results

    Don't Count on Luck

    Calculation questions are always on the project management exam, in three main areas: critical path and duration, activity duration estimates, and earned value analysis. In earned value analysis, CV/EV/AC/SPI/CPI are guaranteed topics. I focused on those, and since past exams always used these five, I gambled and skipped BAC/ETC. Sure enough, what you fear most is what shows up. It appeared in the multiple-choice section of the second-half exam, and I could only pick an answer based on experience. So when reviewing, be thorough and don't count on luck. Every topic you don't want to study could end up on the exam and cost you points.

    Knowledge Matters, but Thinking Matters More

    The afternoon questions are all scenario-based. You get a description and have to identify the problems, answers, and solutions. Based on what the people in the scenario do, you find the key inputs, tools and techniques, and outputs from the nine core knowledge areas. If you only search your memory for what you read in the book, you know the material without really understanding it. The better approach is to start from your own work: when I've faced similar problems at work, how did I handle them? If I haven't, how did colleagues in other departments handle them? Draw your own conclusions and solutions, then compare them against project management knowledge. What did the knowledge areas cover that I missed? Why didn't I think of it? How can I do better next time? Only by thinking deeply, asking questions, and drawing connections can you really put knowledge to use.

    The Predictable and the Unpredictable

    Every exam has patterns. For the project management exam, certain topics and question types will definitely appear, such as the calculation questions mentioned above. Getting those points is simple: understand the textbook thoroughly, find related questions from past exams, practice them together, and analyze every mistake so you don't repeat it.

    The unpredictable parts leave room for flexibility. Make full use of it: bring in your own work experience and insights, and present them logically and clearly. Never ramble just to pad your word count.

    A Small Tip

    Record the topics you tend to get wrong or need to memorize as audio on your phone. Listen to them at intervals based on the Ebbinghaus forgetting curve, and repeat a few times to strengthen your memory. It's a clumsy method, but it works.
